Microcontroller boards such as Arduino run one program directly on a small chip; single-board computers such as the Raspberry Pi are complete computers that run an operating system. Open-source hardware publishes its designs so anyone can build on them.
</Callout>

## Two kinds of boards

IoT projects use two kinds of development boards. They look similar, but they work very differently.

A **microcontroller board** is built around a <Term id="microcontroller">microcontroller</Term>: a processor, memory and input/output pins on one chip. It has no operating system. It runs a single program, called a sketch on Arduino, from the moment it gets power.

A <Term id="sbc">single-board computer (SBC)</Term> is a complete computer on one circuit board. It has a powerful processor, gigabytes of memory and ports for a screen, keyboard and network. It runs an operating system such as Linux, so it can run many programs at once.

<Callout kind="remember">
A microcontroller board is a **worker**: it does one job reliably on very little power. A single-board computer is a **manager**: it runs many programs, talks to the internet and handles heavy work.
</Callout>

### How to choose a board

- **Reading a sensor and switching a relay?** Use a microcontroller board. It is cheap, low-power and instant.
- **Need Wi-Fi or Bluetooth on a small budget?** Use an ESP32 or ESP8266 board.
- **Need a camera, a database, a web dashboard or a gateway?** Use a Raspberry Pi or another SBC.
- **Running AI models, such as recognising objects?** Use a board with a GPU, such as a Jetson.
- **Many real projects use both:** microcontrollers read the sensors and a Raspberry Pi acts as the gateway.

## Pins: how boards talk to sensors

Both kinds of boards have <Term id="gpio">GPIO</Term> pins. A program can set a pin HIGH or LOW to switch an LED, or read it to check a button.

Some pins have extra jobs:

- **Analog input pins** read a voltage through the board's ADC. The Arduino Uno's six analog pins give numbers from 0 to 1023.
- <Term id="pwm">**PWM**</Term> pins switch on and off very quickly to control LED brightness or motor speed. On the Uno these are pins 3, 5, 6, 9, 10 and 11, marked with a ~.
- **Communication pins** support serial (UART), I²C and SPI, which are used by many sensors and displays.

<Callout kind="note" title="Mind the voltage">
Arduino Uno pins work at 5 V. Raspberry Pi and ESP32 pins work at 3.3 V. Connecting a 5 V signal to a 3.3 V pin can damage the board.
</Callout>

## What is open-source hardware?

<Term id="open-source-hardware">Open-source hardware</Term> is hardware whose design files are published. Anyone can study, change, make and sell the design, as long as they follow its licence.

The design files include circuit diagrams (schematics), circuit board layouts and the list of parts (bill of materials). The Open Source Hardware Association (OSHWA) maintains the definition and certifies open designs.

Open hardware uses licences, just like open-source software:

- **CERN Open Hardware Licence (CERN-OHL v2):** written for hardware, in strongly reciprocal, weakly reciprocal and permissive versions.
- **Creative Commons (CC BY-SA):** used for Arduino's board designs.
- **TAPR Open Hardware License:** an older hardware licence.

Even fully open boards usually use closed chips. The microcontroller itself is designed and sold by a chip company.

### Benefits and limits of open hardware

**Benefits**

- Low cost, because many makers can produce compatible boards.
- Easy to learn from, because every detail of the design is visible.
- Easy to customise for a special project.
- A large community shares code, examples and help.

**Limits**

- The chips on the board are usually still closed.
- Quality varies between makers of compatible boards.
- Trademarks still protect brand names.

## Advantages and limitations of development boards

**Advantages**

- Cheap and easy to start with.
- Large libraries of ready-made code for sensors and displays.
- Fast to build and test an idea (prototyping).
- Huge online communities and tutorials.

**Limitations**

- Development boards are larger and costlier than a custom board made for one product.
- Hobby boards are not always designed for harsh industrial conditions.
- SBCs use more power and can corrupt their memory card if power is cut suddenly.
