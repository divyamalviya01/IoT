## What is the Internet of Things?

The <Term id="iot">Internet of Things (IoT)</Term> is a network of physical objects, called "things". Each thing has a <Term id="sensor">sensor</Term>, a small processor and a network connection.

The things collect data and send it over the internet. People and programs use that data to monitor and control the things, often from far away.

A normal computer needs a person to type or click. An IoT device usually works on its own. A smart meter, for example, sends your electricity reading every 15 minutes without anyone pressing a button.

<Callout kind="definition" title="Formal definition (ITU-T Y.2060)">
IoT is "a global infrastructure for the information society, enabling advanced services by interconnecting (physical and virtual) things based on existing and evolving interoperable information and communication technologies."
</Callout>

In simple words: IoT links things together using standard communication technology, so that new services become possible.

## The four building blocks of IoT

Every IoT system, small or large, is built from four blocks. Data moves through them in order, and commands can travel back the other way.

### Things: sensors and actuators

A **sensor** measures something, such as temperature, light or motion. It turns the measurement into an electrical signal.

An <Term id="actuator">actuator</Term> does the opposite. It receives a command and does something physical, such as turning a motor, opening a valve or lighting an LED.

Most things also contain a <Term id="microcontroller">microcontroller</Term>. It reads the sensor, runs simple rules and talks to the network.

### Connectivity

Things rarely connect straight to the internet. They usually talk to a nearby <Term id="gateway">gateway</Term> using a short-range radio such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth or Zigbee.

The gateway then forwards the data to the internet. Each step follows a <Term id="protocol">protocol</Term>, an agreed set of communication rules.

### Data processing

Raw readings are just numbers. Processing turns them into useful decisions, such as "the room is too hot, switch on the fan".

Processing can happen in the <Term id="cloud">cloud</Term>, far away, or close to the devices at the edge. Topic 1.4 compares these choices.

### User interface

The user interface is where people meet the system. It shows live data and history, sends alerts and lets users give commands.

## Real-life example: FASTag

<Callout kind="example" title="FASTag at a toll plaza">
A FASTag sticker on a car's windscreen holds an RFID tag (the thing). A reader at the toll plaza reads it by radio (connectivity). The bank's system checks the account and deducts the toll (data processing). You get an SMS or app notification within seconds (user interface).
</Callout>

All four blocks are present. Take any one away and the service stops working.

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- **Automation:** devices act on their own, saving time and effort.
- **Better decisions:** real data replaces guesswork, for example watering crops only when the soil is dry.
- **Remote control:** you can monitor and control things from anywhere.
- **Efficiency:** less wasted electricity, water and fuel.
- **New services:** remote health monitoring, smart parking and cashless tolls.

**Limitations**

- **Security and privacy:** connected devices can be hacked, and they collect personal data.
- **Interoperability:** devices from different makers may not work together.
- **Dependence on the network:** many services stop when the connection fails.
- **Power:** battery-powered devices need careful energy saving.
- **Cost and complexity:** large systems are expensive to set up and maintain.
