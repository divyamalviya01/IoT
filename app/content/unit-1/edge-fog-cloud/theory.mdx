<Callout kind="definition" title="In one line">
IoT data can be processed on or next to the device (edge), on a nearby local server (fog) or in a distant data centre (cloud). Closer means faster; farther means more computing power.
</Callout>

## Why the place of processing matters

Every reading must be processed somewhere before it becomes a decision. Where that happens affects four things:

- **Speed:** how quickly a decision comes back. This delay is called <Term id="latency">latency</Term>.
- **Bandwidth:** how much data must travel over the internet. Sending raw video from 100 cameras uses a lot of <Term id="bandwidth">bandwidth</Term>.
- **Reliability:** whether the system still works when the internet connection fails.
- **Privacy:** whether personal data has to leave the building.

There are three main places to process data. You can picture them as floors of a building, with the devices on the ground floor.

## Cloud computing

<Term id="cloud">Cloud computing</Term> means using large, shared data centres over the internet. You rent storage and computing power instead of buying your own servers.

The cloud is very good at storing years of data, analysing it and training machine-learning models. It is also easy to reach from anywhere.

Its weakness is distance. Data may travel hundreds of kilometres each way. Sending everything there also costs bandwidth, and the system stops when the internet fails.

## Edge computing

<Term id="edge-computing">Edge computing</Term> processes data on the device itself or on a computer right next to it, sometimes called an edge computer. Only results or summaries go further.

A smart camera that recognises a person and sends only the alert, not the whole video, uses edge computing. So does a car that brakes by itself: it cannot wait for a reply from a distant server.

Edge devices are fast and keep working offline, but they have limited computing power and storage.

## Fog computing

<Term id="fog-computing">Fog computing</Term> sits between the edge and the cloud. It uses local servers, routers or gateways that serve many devices in one area. Cisco introduced the term around 2012.

A factory might send data from 500 machines to a fog server on site. The fog server spots problems in real time and sends only hourly summaries to the cloud.

## The peripheral cloud and cloudlets

The syllabus calls this the **peripheral cloud**: cloud resources placed at the periphery, or edge, of the network, close to the users.

A <Term id="cloudlet">cloudlet</Term> is a small cloud data centre, sometimes called a "data centre in a box", placed near the users. It might sit in a shopping mall, a college campus or a mobile tower.

Mobile operators now place servers at their towers so phones and IoT devices get cloud services with low delay. This is known as multi-access edge computing (MEC).

<Callout kind="remember">
Edge, fog and peripheral cloud all follow one idea: move computing closer to where data is produced. The cloud is still used for long-term storage and heavy analysis.
</Callout>

## Real-life example: smart traffic signals

<Callout kind="example" title="A smart junction in a city">
Cameras at a junction count vehicles on the spot (edge). A controller for the area adjusts signal timings for several nearby junctions together (fog). All traffic data goes to the city's command and control centre, where planners study weeks of data to redesign roads (cloud).
</Callout>
