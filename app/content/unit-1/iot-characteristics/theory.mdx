<Callout kind="definition" title="In one line">
The characteristics of IoT are the features that make an IoT system different from an ordinary computer network: it is huge, mixed, mostly automatic, and made of small devices with limited power.
</Callout>

## Why IoT needs its own list of features

An office network connects a few hundred computers that people use directly. An IoT network may connect millions of small devices that work on their own, outdoors, on batteries.

That difference changes how the network must behave. The devices must set themselves up, adapt to changes, speak common protocols and save energy.

## The five core characteristics in detail

Many textbooks highlight five core characteristics. These often come up in exams.

### Dynamic and self-adapting

IoT devices react to changes around them. They can change how often they report, which mode they work in or which network they use.

A surveillance camera is a good example. It uses normal colour images in daylight and switches to infrared at night. It may also send video only when it detects movement.

### Self-configuring

Setting up millions of devices by hand is impossible. So IoT devices configure themselves: they join a network, get an address and download updates on their own.

When you add a new smart plug, the app finds it, connects it to Wi-Fi and installs the latest <Term id="firmware">firmware</Term> with only a few taps.

### Interoperable communication protocols

<Term id="interoperability">Interoperability</Term> means devices from different makers can work together. This is only possible when they follow the same standard <Term id="protocol">protocols</Term>, such as MQTT, CoAP or HTTP.

Without common protocols, a light switch from one company could not control a bulb from another.

### Unique identity

Every IoT device needs a <Term id="unique-identifier">unique identifier</Term>, so that data comes from, and commands go to, the right device.

Common identifiers are IP addresses, MAC addresses and <Term id="rfid">RFID</Term> codes. <Term id="ipv6">IPv6</Term> uses 128-bit addresses, which allows about 3.4 × 10³⁸ addresses. That is enough for every device on Earth many times over.

### Integrated into the information network

IoT devices do not work alone. They are part of the internet, so their data can be found, shared and combined with data from other systems.

For example, a rain gauge on a farm can share its readings with a district flood-warning service and a crop-insurance app at the same time.

## The other characteristics

- **Connectivity:** data is only useful if it can travel. Devices must stay connected, even when they move or the signal is weak.
- <Term id="scalability">**Scalability:**</Term> the design must handle growth in devices, data and users without slowing down.
- **Heterogeneity:** a real system mixes devices, operating systems and radios. The platform must handle them all.
- **Intelligence:** sensing is not enough. The system must turn raw data into decisions, using rules or machine learning.
- **Limited energy and resources:** most devices have small batteries, little memory and slow processors. Software must be lightweight.
- **Security and privacy:** devices often sit in public places and handle personal data, so they must be protected.

<Callout kind="remember">
The core five are easy to remember as the life of a device: it **adapts** to changes, **configures** itself, **speaks** a common language, **has** a unique name, and **joins** the wider network.
</Callout>

## Real-life example: smart street lights

<Callout kind="example" title="Smart street lights in a smart city">
Thousands of street lights (scalability) from different suppliers (heterogeneity) each have a unique ID. They dim automatically when the road is empty (self-adapting) and new poles join the network on their own (self-configuring). They report faults over standard protocols (interoperability) to the city's control centre, which also uses the data for traffic planning (integration).
</Callout>
