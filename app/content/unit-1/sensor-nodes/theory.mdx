## What is a sensor node?

A sensor node is the basic building block of a wireless sensor network (WSN). Many nodes are spread over an area, such as a farm, a forest or a factory floor.

Each node measures something, such as temperature or soil moisture. It then sends the reading by radio to a gateway, which forwards it to the cloud.

Sensor nodes are usually small, cheap and battery-powered. They must work for months or years without anyone touching them.

- **Sensing unit:** the <Term id="sensor">sensor</Term> measures a physical quantity and gives a voltage. The <Term id="adc">ADC</Term> converts that voltage into a number.
- **Processing unit:** a <Term id="microcontroller">microcontroller</Term> handles the numbers. It can average readings, check limits and decide when to send.
- **Communication unit:** a <Term id="transceiver">transceiver</Term> sends packets out and receives commands.
- **Power unit:** usually a battery. Some nodes add <Term id="energy-harvesting">energy harvesting</Term>, such as a small solar panel.

An <Term id="analog-signal">analog signal</Term> can take any value. A <Term id="digital-signal">digital signal</Term> has only a few fixed values.

## From voltage to number: sampling and quantisation

A microcontroller cannot store a smooth curve. It measures the voltage at regular moments. This is called sampling, and how often it happens is the <Term id="sampling-rate">sampling rate</Term>.

Each sample is rounded to the nearest step the ADC can show. This rounding is called <Term id="quantisation">quantisation</Term>.

An ADC with **n bits** has **2ⁿ steps**. The size of one step is the reference voltage divided by 2ⁿ.

<Callout kind="example" title="Worked example: Arduino Uno and LM35">
The Arduino Uno has a 10-bit ADC with a 5 V reference. It has 2¹⁰ = 1024 steps, so one step is 5 V ÷ 1024 ≈ 4.9 mV. The LM35 gives 10 mV per °C, so one ADC step is about 0.49 °C. The Uno cannot tell apart two temperatures closer than about half a degree.
</Callout>

## Saving energy: duty cycling

The radio uses far more power than anything else. So a node spends most of its time asleep. It wakes up, takes a reading, sends it and sleeps again.

The share of time a node is awake is its <Term id="duty-cycle">duty cycle</Term>. The average current is:

**average current = duty cycle × awake current + (1 − duty cycle) × sleep current**

<Callout kind="example" title="Worked example: battery life">
A node draws 20 mA when awake and 0.01 mA when asleep. It runs on a 2000 mAh battery. Always awake, it lasts 2000 ÷ 20 = 100 hours, about 4 days. With a 1% duty cycle, the average current is 0.01 × 20 + 0.99 × 0.01 ≈ 0.21 mA. It now lasts about 2000 ÷ 0.21 ≈ 9,500 hours, more than a year.
</Callout>

## Real-life example: a farm sensor network

<Callout kind="example" title="Soil sensors on a farm">
Twenty soil-moisture nodes are placed across a sugarcane field. Each wakes every 15 minutes, reads the soil and sends a LoRa packet to a gateway at the farmhouse. A small solar cell keeps each battery charged. The farmer's app shows which parts of the field need water.

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- Small and cheap, so many nodes can cover a large area.
- Wireless, so there is no cabling to install.
- Battery-powered, so they work in remote places.
- Can measure things continuously, day and night.

**Limitations**

- Limited battery life and processing power.
- Short radio range, often needing gateways or relaying.
- Readings drift over time, so sensors need calibration.
- Exposed to weather, theft and tampering.
