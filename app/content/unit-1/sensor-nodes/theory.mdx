## What is a sensor node?

A sensor node is the basic building block of a <Term id="wireless-sensor-network">wireless sensor network (WSN)</Term>. Many nodes are spread over an area, such as a farm, a forest or a factory floor.

Each node measures something, such as temperature or soil moisture. It then sends the reading by radio to a gateway, which forwards it to the cloud.

Sensor nodes are usually small, cheap and battery-powered. They must work for months or years without anyone touching them.

- **Sensing unit:** the <Term id="sensor">sensor</Term> measures a physical quantity and gives a voltage. The <Term id="adc">ADC</Term> converts that voltage into a number.
- **Processing unit:** a <Term id="microcontroller">microcontroller</Term> handles the numbers. It can average readings, check limits and decide when to send.
- **Communication unit:** a <Term id="transceiver">transceiver</Term> sends packets out and receives commands.
- **Power unit:** usually a battery. Some nodes add <Term id="energy-harvesting">energy harvesting</Term>, such as a small solar panel.

The five below come up most often in exams.

**Low power.** Most nodes run on a small battery that nobody changes for months. So every part is chosen to use very little energy. The radio is switched off whenever possible.

**Limited resources.** A node has a slow microcontroller and very little memory. An Arduino Uno has just 2 KB of RAM, while a phone has several gigabytes. So a node's programs must be small and simple.

**Self-organising.** Nodes are often scattered with no fixed plan. A <Term id="self-organising">self-organising</Term> network builds itself: each node finds its neighbours and a path to the gateway. Nobody sets up the links by hand.

**Fault tolerance.** Nodes fail when batteries die, animals chew them or rain gets in. <Term id="fault-tolerance">Fault tolerance</Term> means the network keeps working when some nodes stop. Data simply takes another path through working neighbours.

**Scalability.** A network may start with ten nodes and grow to thousands. <Term id="scalability">Scalability</Term> means new nodes can join without redesigning the network.

<Callout kind="remember" title="Easy way to remember">
Split the list in two. Each **node** is small, cheap, low-power and limited. Together, the **network** organises itself, survives failures and grows.
</Callout>

## From voltage to number

A microcontroller understands only numbers, not voltages. The <Term id="adc">ADC</Term> is the translator between the two.

It does this in three steps.

### Step 1: Sampling

Sampling means measuring the voltage at fixed moments, not all the time. Each measurement is called a sample.

It is like taking a photo every few seconds instead of recording a video.

The <Term id="sampling-rate">sampling rate</Term> is the number of samples per second. It is measured in hertz (Hz). So 10 samples per second is 10 Hz.

Slow things, like room temperature, need only a few samples, such as one a minute. Fast things, like a heartbeat or machine vibration, need many samples every second.

**Rule of thumb:** sample at least twice as fast as the fastest change in the signal.

### Step 2: Quantisation

An ADC cannot show every possible voltage. It has only a fixed number of levels, called steps. So a smooth ramp of voltage becomes a staircase.

Each sample is rounded down to the step just below it. It is like telling your age: at 19 years and 11 months, you still say 19.

This rounding to a step is called <Term id="quantisation">quantisation</Term>. The small amount lost in rounding is called the quantisation error.

The number of steps depends on the ADC's <Term id="adc-resolution">resolution</Term>, which is the number of bits it uses.

- An ADC with **n bits** has **2ⁿ steps**.
- **Step size = reference voltage ÷ 2ⁿ**

The reference voltage is the top of the ADC's range, for example 5 V on an Arduino Uno.

<Callout kind="example" title="Worked example: a 3-bit ADC">
An ADC measures 0–5 V using 3 bits. A sensor gives 2.0 V. Which number does the ADC give?

**1.** Number of steps: 2³ = 8.

**2.** Step size: 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625 V.

**3.** Divide the reading by the step size: 2.0 ÷ 0.625 = 3.2.

**4.** Round down: 3.2 becomes step number **3**.

**5.** Write 3 as a 3-bit binary number: **011**.

So the microcontroller gets 3. It stands for 3 × 0.625 = 1.875 V, a little below the real 2.0 V.
</Callout>

### Step 3: Encoding

Finally, the ADC writes the step number in binary, the language of computers. A 3-bit ADC uses codes from 000 (step 0) to 111 (step 7).

### Why more bits are better

More bits give more steps, so each step is smaller. Smaller steps give a number closer to the real voltage.

## Saving energy: duty cycling

Picture a watchman who naps and wakes every few minutes to check the gate. He still guards the gate, but rests most of the time.

A sensor node does the same. Its radio uses the most power, so the node sleeps most of the time. It wakes, takes a reading, sends it and sleeps again.

The <Term id="duty-cycle">duty cycle</Term> is the share of time the node is awake.

**Duty cycle = awake time ÷ total time**

A node awake 1 second in every 100 seconds has a duty cycle of 1 ÷ 100 = 1%.

Current is measured in milliamps (mA). Battery size is measured in milliamp-hours (mAh). Battery life in hours is the battery size divided by the average current.

Take a node that draws 20 mA awake and 0.01 mA asleep, on a 2000 mAh battery.

<CompareTable
  caption="Duty cycling and battery life"
  columns={["Mode", "Average current", "Battery life on 2000 mAh"]}
  rows={[
    ["Always awake", "20 mA", "2000 ÷ 20 = 100 hours, about 4 days"],
    ["1% duty cycle (awake 20 mA, asleep 0.01 mA)", "≈ 0.21 mA", "2000 ÷ 0.21 ≈ 9,500 hours, more than a year"],
  ]}
/>

Sleeping 99% of the time makes the same battery last about 95 times longer.

<Callout kind="note" title="For exams: average current">
**Average current = duty cycle × awake current + (1 − duty cycle) × sleep current**

For the 1% node: 0.01 × 20 + 0.99 × 0.01 = 0.2 + 0.0099 ≈ 0.21 mA.
</Callout>

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- Small and cheap, so many nodes can cover a large area.
- Wireless, so there is no cabling to install.
- Battery-powered, so they work in remote places.
- Can measure things continuously, day and night.

**Limitations**

- Limited battery life and processing power.
- Short radio range, often needing gateways or relaying.
- Readings drift over time, so sensors need calibration.
- Exposed to weather, theft and tampering.
