<Callout kind="definition" title="In one line">
NFC (Near Field Communication) is a 13.56 MHz radio link that works only over a few centimetres, used for tap-to-pay, smart tags and quick pairing.
</Callout>

## How NFC works

<Term id="nfc">NFC</Term> uses **magnetic induction**. The reader (usually a phone) has a coil that makes a changing magnetic field at **13.56 MHz**. When a tag's coil comes close, the field induces a current in it.

- A **passive tag** has no battery: the reader's field powers its chip, and the tag answers by changing how much power it draws.
- The magnetic field fades very fast with distance. Reads are reliable up to about **4 cm**; about **10 cm** is the most it can manage.
- Data rates are 106, 212 or 424 kbps: plenty for a web address or a payment.

The short range is deliberate. You have to bring the phone or card close on purpose, so a payment or a door unlock cannot happen by accident from across the room.

<Term id="card-emulation">Card emulation</Term> is behind tap-to-pay. The phone stores a token instead of the real card number, and the payment is encrypted, so the short range is only one layer of protection.

## NDEF: the data on a tag

Tags store data in <Term id="ndef">NDEF</Term> (NFC Data Exchange Format). An NDEF message contains one or more **records**, each with a type and a payload:

<CompareTable
  caption="Common NDEF record types"
  columns={["Record", "Type", "Holds", "Example"]}
  rows={[
    ["URI", "U", "A web address, phone number or email", "https://example.com"],
    ["Text", "T", "Plain text with a language code", "\"Room 204, Computer Lab\""],
    ["Smart poster", "Sp", "A URI with a title and action", "A bus stop's live timetable"],
    ["MIME", "media type", "Any data, such as Wi-Fi settings", "Join the guest Wi-Fi by tapping"],
  ]}
/>

To save space, a URI record replaces common beginnings with one byte. For example, code `04` means "https://", so "https://example.com" takes 16 bytes as a record. Tags have little memory: an NTAG213 holds 144 bytes, an NTAG215 504 and an NTAG216 888. Lab L18 shows every byte of a record.

## NFC and RFID

NFC grew out of <Term id="rfid">RFID</Term> (Radio Frequency Identification). RFID comes in several bands with very different ranges:

<Callout kind="remember" title="FASTag is not NFC">
FASTag uses **UHF RFID**, read from several metres away by the toll plaza's overhead reader while the car is moving. NFC works only over a few centimetres, which is why it is used for payments that must be deliberate.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" title="Tap to pay at a kirana shop">
A customer holds a RuPay contactless card or a phone with UPI tap-to-pay near the shop's card machine. The machine's field powers the card's chip, the encrypted payment details pass in a fraction of a second, and small payments go through without a PIN.
</Callout>

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- Very short range makes accidental or remote reads unlikely.
- Passive tags need no battery and cost only a few rupees.
- Instant and simple: tap and done, with no pairing.

**Limitations**

- Works only over a few centimetres, so it cannot be used for sensor networks.
- Low data rate and small tag memory.
- Not every phone has NFC, and some features (such as peer-to-peer) have been removed.
