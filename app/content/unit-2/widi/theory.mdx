## What WiDi was

**WiDi** was Intel's technology, launched in 2010, for mirroring a laptop's screen and sound on a TV or projector without an HDMI cable. It needed an Intel processor and Intel Wi-Fi in the laptop, and a WiDi-ready TV or a small receiver box plugged into the TV.

The laptop and the receiver talked **directly**, peer to peer over Wi-Fi, without going through the home router. That is why the syllabus groups it with wireless technologies.

1. The laptop captures what is on its screen many times a second.
2. It compresses the pictures and sound into a video stream (H.264).
3. The stream travels over a direct Wi-Fi link to the receiver.
4. The receiver decodes the stream and shows it on the TV.

Compression and decoding add a little delay, so mirroring suits slides and videos better than fast games.

## What happened to WiDi

Later versions of WiDi also supported **<Term id="miracast">Miracast</Term>**, an open standard certified by the Wi-Fi Alliance in 2012 and built on <Term id="wifi-direct">Wi-Fi Direct</Term>. Once Windows, Android phones and TVs supported Miracast directly, a separate Intel-only system was no longer needed, and Intel stopped WiDi around 2016.

Google Cast works differently from mirroring: the phone only tells the TV what to play, and the TV streams it straight from the internet, so the phone can be used for other things or even switched off. Google stopped selling Chromecast dongles in 2024, but Cast lives on inside TVs.

<Callout kind="example" title="Presenting in a classroom">
A teacher wants to show slides from a laptop on the classroom TV without hunting for an HDMI cable. With Miracast (on Windows: press Windows + K), the laptop finds the TV and mirrors the screen over a direct Wi-Fi link. Ten years ago, an Intel WiDi receiver box would have done the same job.
</Callout>

## Why it matters for IoT

- Smart TVs and streaming devices are among the most common IoT devices at home.
- WiDi and Miracast show **peer-to-peer Wi-Fi**: two devices linking directly, with no access point.
- The story shows how a single company's technology gives way to an open standard that everyone supports.

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages (of wireless display in general)**

- No cables: present from anywhere in the room.
- Direct link (WiDi and Miracast) works even without a router or internet.

**Limitations**

- Compression adds delay, so fast games and some video can stutter.
- Uses a lot of Wi-Fi bandwidth and power.
- WiDi needed Intel hardware and is now discontinued; Miracast support varies between devices.
