LoRa is a long-range, low-power radio that sends data as chirps; LoRaWAN is the network protocol on top of it, letting battery sensors reach gateways kilometres away for years.
</Callout>

## LPWAN: the problem it solves

Many IoT devices are spread over large areas, such as soil sensors on a farm or water meters across a city. They send only a few bytes now and then, but they must reach far and run for years on a battery. Wi-Fi and Bluetooth cannot reach; mobile data uses too much power and costs a monthly fee.

<Term id="lpwan">Low-Power Wide-Area Networks (LPWAN)</Term> fill this gap. LoRaWAN is the best-known LPWAN that anyone can build without a mobile operator.

## Chirp spread spectrum

<Term id="lora">LoRa</Term> sends data as <Term id="chirp">chirps</Term>: signals whose frequency sweeps steadily from the bottom to the top of the channel. Data is carried by where each chirp starts.

Because each chirp is spread over the whole channel and lasts a known time, the receiver can pick it out even when it is **weaker than the background noise**, down to about 20 dB below the noise at the slowest setting. That is how LoRa reaches kilometres on milliwatts.

## Spreading factor: the main trade-off

The <Term id="spreading-factor">spreading factor (SF)</Term> sets how long each chirp lasts, from SF7 to SF12. Each step up **doubles** the chirp time:

- the receiver can decode a **weaker signal**, so the range grows;
- but the data rate roughly **halves** and each message stays on the air twice as long, which uses more battery.

<Term id="airtime">Time on air</Term> follows Semtech's formula; lab L19 calculates it for any SF, bandwidth and payload. LoRaWAN's **adaptive data rate (ADR)** lets the network move devices near a gateway to a low SF, saving their batteries.

## LoRaWAN architecture: a star of stars

- An end device does not pick a gateway. **Every gateway in range** hears its uplink and forwards it.
- The **network server** throws away the duplicate copies, checks security and decides which gateway sends any reply.
- Gateways are simple packet forwarders, connected by Ethernet or mobile data.

## Airtime rules

Radio rules stop one device from hogging the band:

- In Europe's EU868 band, each device may transmit only **1 %** of the time, about 864 seconds a day. After a message, a device must stay quiet for 99 times its time on air.
- India's **IN865** band (865–867 MHz) has no duty-cycle limit; the rules limit transmit power instead. Shared networks such as **The Things Network** still ask each device to stay under **30 seconds of airtime a day**.

Security uses AES-128 with two keys: a network key that the network server checks, and an application key, so the network operator cannot read the data.

<Callout kind="note" title="Sigfox: another LPWAN">
<Term id="sigfox">Sigfox</Term> uses ultra-narrowband radio run by an operator. A device may send up to 140 messages a day of just 12 bytes each, and receive only 4. The company went bankrupt in 2022 and was bought by UnaBiz. Cellular IoT (NB-IoT and LTE-M) is covered in topic 3.22.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="example" title="Smart farming in India">
A farmer co-operative fits soil-moisture sensors across several villages. Each sensor sends 10 bytes every hour at SF9 or SF10 to a gateway on the panchayat office roof, about 5 km away. With LoRaWAN, a pair of AA batteries lasts several years, and the gateway's 4G link carries the data to the irrigation dashboard.
</Callout>

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- Very long range: kilometres from one gateway.
- Very low power: years on a battery.
- Licence-free band; anyone can set up private gateways.
- Robust radio that works below the noise floor.

**Limitations**

- Very low data rate: a few bytes per message, a few hundred bps at SF12.
- Airtime limits restrict how often devices may send.
- High latency for downlinks in Class A.
- Not suitable for audio, video or real-time control.
