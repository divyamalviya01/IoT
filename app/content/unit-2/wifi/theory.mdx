Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11) gives devices fast wireless IP connections through an access point; it is ideal for cameras and plugged-in devices, but its power use makes it harder for battery sensors.

## What Wi-Fi is

<Term id="wifi">Wi-Fi</Term> is the brand name for wireless networks built on the IEEE 802.11 standards. Devices connect to an <Term id="access-point">access point</Term> (usually inside the home router), which joins them to the wired network and the internet. Every device gets an IP address, so it can talk to cloud services directly.

<Term id="wifi-halow">Wi-Fi HaLow</Term> brings Wi-Fi to IoT: its lower frequency reaches about a kilometre and passes walls better, at lower data rates and power.

## Bands and channels

- **2.4 GHz:** longer range and better through walls, but crowded. Microwave ovens, Bluetooth and Zigbee use it too.
- **5 GHz:** many more channels and faster, but shorter range.
- **6 GHz:** the newest band (Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7), with plenty of clean channels, being opened for Wi-Fi in more and more countries.

In the 2.4 GHz band, India allows channels 1 to 13. Channel centres are only **5 MHz apart**, but each channel is about **20–22 MHz wide**, so neighbouring channels overlap. Only channels **1, 6 and 11** are far enough apart not to overlap.

This is called <Term id="co-channel">co-channel and adjacent-channel interference</Term>. The Visualize tab lets you place access points on channels and see the clashes.

<Callout kind="remember" title="1, 6 and 11">
In 2.4 GHz, use channels 1, 6 and 11. Some guides suggest 1, 5, 9 and 13, but that works only when every device uses 20 MHz-wide channels, so 1, 6 and 11 is the safe rule. Bluetooth Low Energy places its advertising channels in the gaps between Wi-Fi channels 1, 6 and 11.
</Callout>

## Wi-Fi and power in IoT

Wi-Fi was built for speed, not batteries. Joining a network and staying connected costs a lot of energy, so most Wi-Fi IoT devices are plugged in. Battery Wi-Fi devices use tricks:

- **Power-save mode:** the device sleeps between beacons from the access point.
- **Target Wake Time (Wi-Fi 6):** with <Term id="twt">TWT</Term>, the device agrees with the access point when it will next wake, then sleeps until then.
- **Deep sleep:** the device wakes, connects, sends a reading and powers down. It works, but connecting takes time and energy.
- **HaLow:** a lower-power, longer-range version of Wi-Fi for sensors.

Cheap Wi-Fi microcontrollers such as the ESP8266 and ESP32 made Wi-Fi the easiest way for hobbyists and start-ups to connect a device to the internet.

## Security

Wi-Fi networks should use **WPA2** or, better, **WPA3** encryption. IoT devices are often the weakest link, so change default passwords and keep firmware up to date.

<Callout kind="example" title="A smart plug and a CCTV camera">
A smart plug at home uses an ESP8266 to join the Wi-Fi and talk to its maker's cloud, so you can switch it from anywhere. It is always plugged in, so Wi-Fi's power use does not matter. A CCTV camera needs several Mbps for video, which only Wi-Fi among IoT radios can carry.
</Callout>

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- High data rates: video, firmware updates and large files.
- Devices speak IP directly, with no special gateway.
- Already present in almost every home, office and phone.

**Limitations**

- High power use: hard for small batteries.
- The 2.4 GHz band is crowded and has only three non-overlapping channels.
- Range is limited to tens of metres indoors (except HaLow).
- Each access point can handle only so many devices well.
