<Callout kind="definition" title="In one line">
A point-to-point link joins exactly two devices directly; when many devices must connect, they are arranged in a pattern called a topology, such as star, mesh, tree, bus or ring.
</Callout>

## Who talks to whom

Every IoT system has to decide how its devices are joined. There are four basic ways to send a message:

<CompareTable
  caption="Ways to send a message"
  columns={["Way", "What happens", "IoT example"]}
  rows={[
    ["Point-to-point", "One device sends to exactly one other device over a direct link", "A sensor wired to an Arduino; a phone paired with a fitness band"],
    ["Point-to-multipoint", "One central device talks to many devices, one link each", "A Wi-Fi router serving many smart plugs"],
    ["Broadcast", "One message goes to every device at once", "A gateway announcing the time to every node"],
    ["Mesh", "Devices pass messages for each other, hop by hop", "Zigbee bulbs relaying commands across a house"],
  ]}
/>

A <Term id="point-to-point">point-to-point link</Term> is the simplest: two ends, nothing else sharing the link, no addresses needed. It is easy to build and secure, but it does not scale, because every new pair needs its own link.

## Network topologies

When more than two devices connect, their arrangement is called the <Term id="topology">topology</Term>. In IoT, the devices usually send their readings to a gateway. Each step a message takes is one <Term id="hop">hop</Term>.

<CompareTable
  caption="Topologies compared"
  columns={["Topology", "Shape", "Advantages", "Limitations", "IoT example"]}
  rows={[
    ["Star", "Every device links to a central hub", "Simple, one hop to the hub, one bad device affects only itself", "The hub is a single point of failure; range limited by the hub", "Wi-Fi devices around a router; BLE sensors around a phone"],
    ["Mesh", "Devices link to several neighbours", "Many routes, so it survives failures; extends range hop by hop", "More links and routing work; more power for routers", "Zigbee and Thread smart homes"],
    ["Tree", "Devices hang off parents in branches", "Easy to grow and organise in levels", "A broken branch cuts off everything below it", "Sensor networks with cluster heads"],
    ["Bus", "All devices share one cable", "Cheapest wiring, easy to add a device", "One cable break splits the network; devices take turns", "Modbus devices on RS-485; CAN bus in cars"],
    ["Ring", "Each device links to the next in a loop", "Survives one break by going the other way", "Two breaks split it; long trips round the loop", "Some factory and fibre networks"],
  ]}
/>

A part whose failure stops everything is a <Term id="single-point-of-failure">single point of failure</Term>. The hub of a star is one. A mesh avoids them by giving each device more than one route.

<Callout kind="remember" title="More links, more strength">
Adding links costs money and power, but it removes single points of failure. A star has the fewest links and a full mesh the most. Lab L12 lets you fail nodes and links to see who gets cut off.
</Callout>

## Wired point-to-point links on a board

Inside an IoT device, chips talk to each other over short wired links. Four are worth knowing:

<CompareTable
  caption="Short-range wired links"
  columns={["Link", "Wires", "Duplex", "Shape", "Typical use"]}
  rows={[
    ["UART", "2 (TX, RX) + ground", "Full", "Point-to-point", "Microcontroller to GPS or GSM module, serial monitor"],
    ["I²C", "2 (data SDA, clock SCL)", "Half", "Bus with addresses, 100 or 400 kbit/s", "Many sensors on one board, such as BMP280 and OLED displays"],
    ["SPI", "4 (MOSI, MISO, clock, chip select)", "Full", "One controller, chips chosen by a select wire", "SD cards, fast displays, radio chips"],
    ["RS-485", "2 (a twisted pair)", "Half", "Bus, up to 32 devices, about 1200 m", "Modbus meters and factory machines"],
  ]}
/>

<Term id="uart">UART</Term> has no clock wire. Both ends agree on a speed, the <Term id="baud-rate">baud rate</Term>, such as 9600 bits per second. Each character travels as a frame:

1. The line rests high (1).
2. A **start bit** (0) tells the receiver a character is coming.
3. **Eight data bits** follow, lowest bit first.
4. An optional **<Term id="parity-bit">parity bit</Term>** helps detect an error.
5. A **stop bit** (1) ends the frame.

So 8 data bits cost 10 bits on the wire, and 9600 baud carries at most 960 characters a second. The Visualize tab animates a frame.

<Term id="i2c">I²C</Term> and <Term id="spi">SPI</Term> add a clock wire, so they can go faster and do not need an agreed speed. <Term id="rs-485">RS-485</Term> sends each bit as the difference between two wires, which cancels electrical noise over long factory cables.

<Callout kind="example" title="A smart home uses several at once">
Inside a smart switch, the microcontroller talks to its relay driver over I²C. The switch joins a Zigbee mesh with the other switches. The Zigbee hub sits in a star around the Wi-Fi router. One building, three kinds of link.
</Callout>

## Advantages and limitations of point-to-point links

**Advantages**

- Simple: no addresses, no sharing and no collisions.
- Private and secure, because only two devices use the link.
- Full speed of the link for one pair.

**Limitations**

- Does not scale: n devices need many separate links to all talk directly (a full mesh of n devices needs n(n − 1)/2 links).
- Each device needs a separate port or radio for each partner.
- No relaying, so range is limited to the link itself.
