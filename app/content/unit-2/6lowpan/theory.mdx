<Callout kind="definition" title="In one line">
6LoWPAN (IPv6 over Low-power Wireless Personal Area Networks) is an adaptation layer that squeezes IPv6 packets into tiny IEEE 802.15.4 radio frames by compressing headers and splitting large packets.
</Callout>

## The problem 6LoWPAN solves

Every IoT device should ideally have its own internet address, and IPv6 has enough for billions of things. But IPv6 was designed for big networks:

- An IPv6 header alone is **40 bytes**, and UDP adds **8 more**.
- IPv6 expects links to carry packets of at least **1280 bytes**.
- An <Term id="ieee-802-15-4">IEEE 802.15.4</Term> radio frame carries at most **127 bytes**, and up to 25 of those go to its own header and error check.

Without help, the headers would eat half of every frame, and most IPv6 packets would not fit at all. 6LoWPAN, defined by the IETF in RFC 4944 and RFC 6282, fixes this.

## 1. Header compression

<Term id="header-compression">Header compression</Term> leaves out anything the receiver can work out for itself:

- The IP version is always 6.
- Traffic class and flow label are usually zero.
- The payload length can be read from the frame length.
- The source and destination addresses can be built from the radio's own MAC addresses.
- Common hop-limit values and UDP ports can be sent in a few bits.

With the IPHC scheme, the 48 bytes of IPv6 and UDP headers shrink to as few as **6 bytes**: 2 bytes of IPHC, 1 byte saying UDP follows, 1 byte for both port numbers and the 2-byte UDP checksum.

## 2. Fragmentation

If a packet is still too big, 6LoWPAN uses <Term id="fragmentation">fragmentation</Term>: it splits the packet across several frames and the receiver puts them back together.

- The **first fragment** has a 4-byte header (the whole packet's size and a tag) and carries the IP and UDP headers.
- **Later fragments** have a 5-byte header that adds an offset, counted in units of 8 bytes.
- Every fragment except the last carries a multiple of 8 bytes.

<CompareTable

Compression matters most for small packets, which is what sensors usually send: at 80 bytes it halves the frames. For big payloads both need about the same number of frames, but compression still saves bytes. The Visualize tab has a calculator for any payload.

<Callout kind="remember" title="Losing one fragment loses the packet">
If one fragment is lost, the whole packet must be sent again. That is why IoT protocols keep messages small enough to fit in one frame whenever they can.
</Callout>

## 3. Mesh-under and route-over

In a multi-hop network, packets must be forwarded from radio to radio. 6LoWPAN allows two ways:

<Term id="rpl">RPL</Term> builds a tree of routes towards the network's root, which is usually the border router.

## The border router

A 6LoWPAN network joins the wider internet through a <Term id="edge-router">border (edge) router</Term>. It compresses and expands headers, routes packets between the radio mesh and Wi-Fi or Ethernet, and often acts as the RPL root.

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- Gives tiny battery devices real IPv6 addresses, so they can be reached end to end.
- Compression saves bytes, airtime and battery.
- Works with standard internet tools and protocols such as CoAP.

**Limitations**

- Fragmentation is fragile: losing one fragment means resending the whole packet.
- Needs a border router to reach other networks.
- Low data rate (250 kbps) and small frames limit what can be sent.
