## What is this lab?

The IoT Simulator Lab is a <Term id="simulation">simulation</Term>. It behaves like real IoT devices and networks, but it runs on your computer.

You do not need sensors, boards or wires. You can repeat an experiment as often as you like. The same settings always give the same result, so your observations will match your classmates' observations.

Real hardware still matters. Use this lab to understand the ideas first. Your hardware practicals will then make much more sense.

## The journey of one reading

Most IoT systems move data in the same way. A <Term id="sensor">sensor</Term> measures something, such as room temperature. The reading then passes through a few stops until it reaches an app on your phone.

The reading does not travel as one long stream. It is split into small <Term id="packet">packets</Term>. Each packet carries the useful data, called the <Term id="payload">payload</Term>, plus a label saying where it is going.

The <Term id="gateway">gateway</Term> is the bridge between the sensor and the internet. In a home, your Wi-Fi router often does this job.

## Two words you will meet everywhere

Every link on the journey has two important properties: how long it takes, and how reliable it is.

<CompareTable
  caption="Latency and packet loss"
  columns={["Term", "Meaning", "Everyday example", "Measured in"]}
  rows={[
    ["Latency", "Time a packet takes to cross a link", "Time a courier takes to deliver a parcel", "Milliseconds (ms)"],
    ["Packet loss", "Share of packets that never arrive", "Parcels lost on the way", "Percent (%)"],
  ]}
/>

<Term id="latency">Latency</Term> adds up along the path. If each of three links takes 100 ms, the whole journey takes about 300 ms.

<Term id="packet-loss">Packet loss</Term> adds up too, but less obviously. A packet must survive every link. With 10% loss on each of three links, only 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9, about 73%, of packets arrive.

<Callout kind="remember">
More links mean more delay and more chances to lose a packet. That is why IoT designers try to keep the path from sensor to cloud short.
</Callout>

## A conversation between devices

Devices often confirm that a message arrived. The receiver sends back a short reply called an acknowledgement, or "ack". If no ack arrives in time, the sender tries again.

Press **Next step** to watch a sensor send two readings. The second reading is lost, so the sensor sends it again.

## How to do a lab and print your record

1. Read the aim and the steps at the top of the lab.
2. Change the settings in the Setup panel.
3. Press the play button and watch the simulation run.
4. When a run ends, press **Record observation**. A new row appears in the observation table.
5. Repeat with different settings, changing one thing at a time.
6. Press **Open lab record**, add your name and your result, then print it or save it as a PDF.

Try it now in the **Lab** tab. Lab L00 sends packets from a sensor to the cloud, and you control the delay and the loss.
