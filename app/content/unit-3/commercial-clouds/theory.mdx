The big cloud providers sell ready-made IoT platforms that connect, identify and manage millions of devices and route their data to storage, analytics and apps.

## What a cloud IoT platform does

Connecting one sensor to a server is easy. Connecting a lakh of sensors securely, keeping track of each one and acting on their data in real time is hard. A cloud IoT platform does this work as a service (PaaS).

The features they all share:

- **<Term id="device-registry">Device registry</Term>:** a list of every device with its identity and credentials, usually an X.509 certificate.
- **Device gateway:** accepts connections over MQTT, HTTPS and sometimes AMQP, at very large scale.
- **<Term id="rules-engine">Rules engine</Term>:** checks each message and sends it on, for example "if temperature > 40, store it and send an alert".
- **<Term id="device-shadow">Device shadow or twin</Term>:** the device's last known state and the state the app wants, kept in the cloud.
- **Edge runtime:** software that runs the same rules on a local gateway when the internet is slow or down.
- **Security and provisioning:** encryption, certificates and a way to register thousands of new devices automatically.

<Callout kind="remember" title="The lesson: avoid lock-in">
Use standard protocols such as MQTT, keep your data in formats you can export, and keep device firmware able to change its server address. Then moving to another platform is a project, not a disaster.
</Callout>

## Device shadow and device twin

Devices are often offline: asleep to save battery, or out of signal. An app cannot wait for them. So the cloud keeps a **shadow** (AWS and Alibaba call it this) or **twin** (Azure) for each device.

- **Desired:** the state the app wants, such as `"power": "on"`.
- **Reported:** the state the device last said it is in.
- **Delta:** the difference. The device reads it when it next connects, applies it and reports back.
- **Version:** goes up on every change, so old updates can be ignored.

```json
{
  "state": {
    "desired":  { "power": "on" },
    "reported": { "power": "off" },
    "delta":    { "power": "on" }
  },
  "version": 7
}
```

The Visualize tab lets you switch a bulb while it is offline and watch the delta wait in the cloud.

A <Term id="digital-twin">digital twin</Term> is a bigger idea: a live model of a whole machine, building or city that is kept up to date with sensor data and used to test "what if" changes. A device shadow is the simplest kind of twin.

<Callout kind="example" title="Smart street lights">
A city fits 20,000 street lights with controllers that report over mobile data. A rule in the cloud turns on the lights at sunset by setting each shadow's desired state to "on". Lights that are offline that evening pick up the change the moment they reconnect. All three big providers have data centres in India, so the city can keep its data inside the country.
</Callout>

## Choosing a platform

- Which protocols and device types it supports.
- Where its data centres are, for speed and data-protection laws.
- What you pay as the number of devices and messages grows.
- How easy it is to export your data and devices if you leave.
- The skills your team already has.

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- Scales from ten devices to millions without new hardware.
- Security, certificates and provisioning are built in.
- Joins easily with the provider's storage, analytics and AI services.

**Limitations**

- Lock-in: each platform's rules, shadows and SDKs are different.
- Services can be retired, forcing a move.
- Message-based billing can become costly at scale.
- Needs internet access, unless an edge runtime is used.
