<Callout kind="definition" title="In one line">
Open-source IoT platforms are free software you can download, run on your own computer or server and change, so you are not tied to one cloud provider.
</Callout>

## Why open source for IoT?

Commercial clouds (topic 3.3) are easy to start with, but you pay per message and depend on one company. Open-source platforms give another choice:

- **No licence fee:** download and use them freely.
- **Run anywhere:** a Raspberry Pi in a lab, a college server, a private cloud or a public cloud.
- **Your data stays with you:** useful for privacy, data-protection laws and places with poor internet.
- **Change the code:** add a protocol or feature you need.
- **No lock-in:** if one tool stops being maintained, you or the community can carry on.

The catch: **you** must install, secure, back up and update the platform.

<Callout kind="example" title="Campus energy monitor">
A college fits energy meters in its labs. An ESP32 in each lab publishes readings to Mosquitto on a Raspberry Pi. Node-RED turns off idle air conditioners at 6 pm and sends an alert if a lab uses power at night. Grafana shows each lab's use on the notice-board screen. Everything runs on campus, and the software cost is zero.
</Callout>

## Advantages and limitations

**Advantages**

- No licence fee and no per-message bill.
- Full control of data; can run without the internet.
- Code can be studied and changed; large communities share help and add-ons.
- No lock-in to one provider.

**Limitations**

- You must host, secure, monitor, back up and update it yourself.
- Help comes from forums, unless you buy support.
- Scaling to millions of devices takes skill.
- Licence duties (for example AGPL) and licence changes must be watched.
