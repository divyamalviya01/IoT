## Four questions of threat modelling

A <Term id="threat-model">threat model</Term> answers four questions:

1. **What are we working on?** Draw the system.
2. **What can go wrong?** List threats, for example with STRIDE.
3. **What are we going to do about it?** Rate each risk and choose defences.
4. **Did we do a good job?** Check, test and update the model when the system changes.

## Step 3: rate each risk

Rate likelihood and impact from 1 (very low) to 5 (very high). **Risk = likelihood × impact**, placed on a <Term id="risk-matrix">risk matrix</Term>.

<CompareTable
  caption="Risk bands on a 5 × 5 matrix"

  rows={[
    ["1–4", "Low", "Accept, or fix when convenient"],
    ["5–9", "Medium", "Plan a fix"],
    ["10–16", "High", "Fix before release"],
    ["20–25", "Critical", "Fix immediately; do not launch"],
  ]}
/>

## Step 4: choose countermeasures

For each risk you can **reduce** it (add a control), **avoid** it (remove the feature), **transfer** it (for example insurance) or **accept** it (if it is low). A control must match the threat: encryption stops information disclosure, but does nothing against spoofing, which needs authentication.

## Threat analysis in IoT-A

The IoT-A reference model includes a threat analysis. It lists threats to each part of its architecture (devices, communication, services and users) and maps them to its security functions: authentication, authorisation, identity management, key exchange, and trust and reputation. STRIDE is a widely used method for carrying out such an analysis on a real design.

<Callout kind="example" title="Smart lock: the top risk">
The highest risk in the smart-lock model is someone signing in as the resident with a stolen password (likelihood 4 × impact 5 = 20, critical). Two-factor login cuts the likelihood to 2, giving 10 (high). A second authentication control lowers it to 5 (medium). The Visualize tab and lab L28 let you try this.
</Callout>
