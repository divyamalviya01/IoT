<Callout kind="definition" title="In one line">
Lightweight cryptography is encryption designed to run on tiny devices, with little memory, a slow processor and a small battery, while still being strong enough to trust.
</Callout>

## Why ordinary cryptography can be too heavy

Laptops and phones have fast processors and AES built into the chip. Many IoT devices do not.

On the smallest devices, a standard cipher may use too much memory, take too long or drain the battery. <Term id="lightweight-cryptography">Lightweight cryptography</Term> is designed for them.

## What makes a cipher lightweight

- **Small code and RAM:** fits in a few kilobytes.
- **Few logic gates:** cheap to build into a chip or tag.
- **Simple operations:** additions, rotations and XORs that small processors do quickly.
- **Smaller blocks:** some use 64-bit blocks instead of 128.
- **One pass does two jobs:** authenticated encryption (<Term id="aead">AEAD</Term>) encrypts and adds an integrity tag together.

## The main lightweight algorithms

    ["AES with hardware support", "Block cipher", "128-bit block", "Very fast when the chip has an AES engine (for example ESP32)", "FIPS 197"],

- <Term id="present-cipher">PRESENT</Term> was one of the first ciphers built for very small chips.
- <Term id="speck">SIMON and SPECK</Term> are efficient, but many experts did not trust their origin, so ISO did not standardise them.
- <Term id="chacha20">ChaCha20</Term> is why many phones and Raspberry Pi 4 boards (no AES instructions) use it instead of AES.
- <Term id="ascon">Ascon</Term> won NIST's lightweight cryptography competition in February 2023. NIST SP 800-232 (August 2025) standardises Ascon-AEAD128 for encryption, Ascon-Hash256 for hashing, and two extendable-output functions.

## Public keys on small devices: ECC beats RSA

Public-key cryptography is the heaviest part, so key size matters. <Term id="ecc">Elliptic-curve cryptography</Term> needs far smaller keys than RSA for the same strength.

On an 8-bit microcontroller, an ECC key exchange takes about a second or two, while the equivalent RSA private-key operation can take a minute or more. That is why IoT protocols prefer ECC (for example X25519 and ECDSA).

## How much does it cost?

Lab L27 estimates time and energy per message for each algorithm on an Arduino Uno, an ESP32 and a Raspberry Pi 4. Its figures are illustrative, but they show the real patterns:

- On the 8-bit Uno, SPECK and Ascon are much cheaper than AES-GCM in software.
- On the ESP32, AES-GCM wins because the chip has AES hardware.
- On the Pi 4, ChaCha20 beats AES, because its CPU lacks AES instructions.
- Encrypting a short message costs far less energy than **sending** it by radio, so skipping encryption saves almost nothing.

## Choosing a cipher for an IoT device

1. Prefer **authenticated encryption** (Ascon, AES-GCM, ChaCha20-Poly1305), never encryption alone.
2. Use **AES** if the chip has AES hardware; otherwise consider **Ascon** or **ChaCha20**.
3. Use **ECC**, not RSA, for keys and signatures.
4. Use standardised algorithms and tested libraries; never invent your own cipher.

<Callout kind="example" title="A soil sensor on two AA cells">
A farm sensor with an 8-bit microcontroller sends 20 bytes every 15 minutes over LoRaWAN. Protecting each message with Ascon takes well under a millisecond and about 0.02 millijoules, while sending it by radio takes around 10 millijoules or more. Strong encryption barely changes the battery life.
</Callout>
